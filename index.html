The Chemistry of Affordable Skincare — and Why the Price of “Pretty” Is Mostly Marketing

Skincare, Hygiene & Self-Care

Central Research Question: Do low-income consumers have to compromise on skincare quality? Or is the difference between “affordable” and “luxury” skincare an artificial difference created by marketing instead of chemistry?

Thesis: Across the three main skincare products, cleanser, moisturizer and sunscreen, the active ingredients responsible for changes to your skin are nearly identical from the drugstore to the department store. For sunscreen, this is actually mandated by law.

The difference in price between drugstore and prestige versions mostly goes into packaging, scent, texture, branding, and logo. It does not usually go into better active ingredients. The one actual disadvantage to low-income consumers is not in the ingredients per se, but rather in the availability of a well-made and safe product at a shelf that they are actually able to reach.

The Argument, in Plain Terms

A $1 per ounce drugstore sunscreen and a $48 prestige product at the department store can legally contain the same UV filters present in the same concentration. Likewise, a $1 per ounce drugstore moisturizer can contain the complete ceramide-cholesterol-fatty acid complex that dermatologists do support, while a prestige $200 moisturizer turns out to not contain this. A $0.92 per ounce drugstore cleanser and a $22 per ounce prestige cleanser can be created with the same mild coconut-derived surfactant.

None of this is a coincidence, nor is it hidden. It can be found on the back of each product. In this paper we will analyze the ingredients listed and see where the extra money goes when it is not going into better ingredients.

The positive aspect to this article is straightforward and once you understand it, you will not forget it. There are some compounds that maintain the health and beauty of the skin and they are among the most commonly used, well studied, widely available and inexpensive compounds in the consumer products industry. These compounds do not require money in order to be used. Instead, one skill is required - reading the ingredient label and that skill is free. After reading this article, you will have acquired that skill.

How to Read a Skincare Label (a Two-Minute Chemistry Primer)

Skincare products can be divided into two components: actives and vehicle.

  • Actives are the ingredients that perform the function listed on the product. They provide UV protection, replenish lipids in the skin, and remove excess oil. These are ingredients that have published research behind them. Also, these are often only a small portion of the total ingredients.
  • The rest of the ingredients (excipients and base) are things like water, preservatives, fragrances, thickeners, and texture agents that determine whether the product feels like a prestige cream or an inexpensive lotion. The base is where much of the sensory experience lies. Most of the sensory experience is also what the prestige price is actually selling.

This is the most useful breakdown in this paper. A product can contain similar levels of actives between a drugstore and a prestige product but can differ drastically in the base. Most of the price difference between drugstore and prestige products lies in the base. It is in the smooth silk-like texture, the whipped feel, or the melting in of the product. These are pleasant to many people, but not part of the skin health or improvement provided by skincare.

Why the Ingredient Names Look So Scary

All skincare contains an ingredient list. These ingredients may well look like a chemistry exam that you have not studied for. They include: butyl methoxydibenzoylmethane, tocopherol, sodium cocoyl isethionate, Chamomilla recutita flower extract etc. But this does not mean that the manufacturer is trying to intimidate you. All skincare ingredients must be listed using a standard dictionary known as INCI (International Nomenclature of Cosmetic Ingredients). INCI ensures that all ingredients are listed in the same manner by all manufacturers. This allows a consumer, doctor, allergist or any person from any country to identify the contents of the product.

There are a few types of INCI names and there is a pattern that helps demystify most of these.

  • Chemical names for synthesized molecules (butyl methoxydibenzoylmethane is just the formal name for avobenzone).
  • Plant-derived ingredients are listed by their Latin names (Butyrospermum parkii is shea butter; Aloe barbadensis is aloe). “Scary Latin” usually refers to a plant that you might know.
  • Vitamins are listed by their chemical names (tocopherol is vitamin E; ascorbic acid is vitamin C; retinol is a form of vitamin A).

A long name is not always “bad.” It is most of the time just the exact name that is required by law for a common ingredient. The “scary” aspect is due to the translation and not the ingredient itself.

The Order Tells You the Dose — and the “1% Line”

Two rules make the rest of this paper easy to follow:

  • Ingredients are listed according to how much is present. The ingredients listed first are present in the greatest amounts and the ingredients listed at the end are present in much smaller amounts (often less than a percent). If two products have the same ingredient at the same location then they are likely to have similar amounts of that ingredient.
  • The product cost is not part of the molecule’s awareness. The purified niacinamide in a $7 product is identical to the niacinamide in a $70 product. There is no “luxury” glycerin. In this case, the chemistry is the leveler and that’s what we want.

There is a shorthand that formulators and label-readers can use called the 1% line. Ingredients present in an amount greater than 1% need to be in order of amount. Any ingredients present at 1% or less however can be listed in any order. In practice this 1% line usually falls around the preservatives and fragrance (phenoxyethanol, fragrance/parfum, etc). One rule of thumb is that once you reach an ingredient like fragrance or a preservative most of what follows is present in very small amounts. This is the reason why an ingredient like a “hero” botanical might be featured on the front of the box but listed at the end of the ingredient list. It is present in very small amounts for the purpose of marketing this ingredient. Reading the ingredient list will also let you know if an ingredient is actually performing a purpose or is just there to decorate.

What “Fragrance” Legally Hides

There is one ingredient that deserves a special call-out: fragrance (or parfum). Under longstanding trade secrecy, companies can list a scent with dozens of different aroma components under a single word without listing any of those components. This is legal and that is the reason “fragrance” is one of the most commonly listed ingredients that also tells you the least about the contents of the product.

This has two practical applications. First, fragrance is one of the more common causes of contact allergy or skin irritation. As a result of this if you have a reactive skin then “fragrance free” can be a valuable attribute to look for (note: “unscented” does not necessarily mean “fragrance free.” It may mean that a fragrance has been added in order to mask a smell). Second and for this paper’s thesis: fragrance is a cost that the luxury tier leans on heavily. Part of the appeal of luxury skincare is a strong and expensive fragrance. This is sensory theater and it does nothing for the health of the skin. The cheap products that come “fragrance free” may in fact be kinder to the skin. They may just be less harsh.

pH and the Skin’s Acid Mantle

Skin is not pH neutral. The surface of the skin does possess an acid mantle. The pH of the skin is approximately between 4.7 and 5.5. This slightly acidic pH is not without purpose. It plays a role in the proper assembly of the barrier lipids and also assists in the maintenance of the beneficial microflora found upon healthy skin. It can also inhibit the growth of some unwanted micro-organisms.

Products that are very far outside of this pH range can damage the acid mantle. This is one of the key points in the cleanser section. A well formulated affordable cleanser is often designed to clean near the skin’s natural pH while traditional soaps (even expensive soaps) can be alkaline enough to lead to tight and dry skin. pH is a chemistry that is invisible and is not typically labeled on a product. However, it is also one of the areas where “how well formulated is this” can be decided and has nothing to do with price.

Two Last Notes: Patch-Testing, and Why “Natural” Isn’t a Chemistry Word

Patch test new products. Before applying a new product to the whole face (such as a retinoid, an exfoliating acid or a product containing vitamin C), patch test first by placing a small amount on a small, inconspicuous area of skin (by the jawline or on the inner forearm). Everyone’s skin is different and this simple practice can catch any skin irritation before it becomes a problem a week later. Skin science is universal but your skin is unique.

A final myth-buster: “natural” is not a science-based attribute. There is no regulatory definition that guarantees a “natural” product is safer, gentler or more effective. Poison ivy is natural and petrolatum (one of the most effective and best tolerated skin ingredients available) is refined from petroleum. Your skin responds to dose and structure of a compound and not to where it came from or to the leaf on a product. Do not let “natural,” “clean” or “chemical free” (which is not even possible since water is a chemical) make decisions for you. The ingredient list should make those decisions for you.

Primer: A product contains both actives and a base. Ingredients are listed according to an established chemical naming system called INCI. When reading ingredients be mindful of ingredient concentration (read from top to bottom), the 1% line and the fact that “fragrance” hides a lot of information and “natural” says nothing about the product. These are the key points. The rest of this paper will be practice.

1. Sunscreen — The Cleanest Case, Because the Law Makes Them Equal

Sunscreen is one of the best examples that supports the “artificial inequality” side of the thesis. This is because in the United States sunscreen is regulated as a drug and not as a cosmetic. This means the difference in an affordable and a prestige sunscreen is limited.

First, What UV Light Actually Does to Skin

In order to understand sunscreen ingredients we need to understand why they are regulated so strictly. Sunlight has ultraviolet (UV) radiation. This UV radiation can be broken up into bands measured in nanometers (nm) of wavelength:

  • UVB (~290–320 nm) is the shorter, higher energy band. This portion of UV is absorbed at the surface and is the primary cause of sunburn. Also UVB can be absorbed by the DNA present in the skin leading to the kind of DNA damage that is the primary cause of most skin cancers. UVB is the “burning and cancer” radiation.
  • UVA2 (~320–340 nm) and UVA1 (~340–400 nm) are longer wavelengths. UVA penetrates deeper into the skin compared to UVB and is present all year long and throughout the day even through clouds and glass. UVA is the primary cause of photoaging (sagging, mottled pigmentation, and wrinkling that people spend money on to try and reverse) and also plays a role in skin cancer. UVA is the “aging and deeper damage” radiation.

“Broad-spectrum” on a sunscreen means that the sunscreen will protect from both families of UV radiation (UVB and UVA). This will turn out to be the hidden key in the sunscreen discussion.

What SPF Actually Measures (and Where the Math Stops Rewarding You)

SPF is Sun Protection Factor. SPF is not as broad as most people believe. SPF is actually primarily a measure of UVB protection. It is defined as how much longer it takes to cause erythema (visible reddening of the skin) with SPF on the skin compared to without SPF. This threshold is called the Minimal Erythema Dose (MED). SPF does not on its own tell much about UVA protection.

And the SPF number climbs on a curve of sharply diminishing returns:

SPF Approximate share of UVB blocked
SPF 15 ~93%
SPF 30 ~97%
SPF 50 ~98%

Pay close attention. The jump from SPF 15 to SPF 30 adds about 4 percentage points of UVB protection. The jump from SPF 30 to SPF 50 adds about one more point. No filter can block 100%. The jump from an affordable SPF 30 to a prestige SPF 50 is insignificant in terms of UVB protection. It is a rounding error that you pay a premium for. This is the first indication that the value in sunscreen does not lie in the number on the front of the product.

The Toolbox: a Short, Shared List of Filters

The FDA has a list of allowed actives in sunscreen called a monograph. This is a legally binding list of allowed UV filters. For many years there were 16 (14 organic and 2 mineral). In June 2026 the FDA approved a 17th UV filter (bemotrizinol). This is the first new UV filter approved since 1999. All sunscreens sold in the United States (from drugstore to prestige) must use these same 17 filters. There is no extra filter available to be bought.

This point goes further than it first appears. Until 2026 better UV filters were not available anywhere in the United States. Bemotrizinol has been available in Europe and Asia (under the name Tinosorb S) since about 1999–2000. For over 20 years more effective UVA filters used in Europe have been illegal to sell here in either an affordable or prestige product (at either $5 or $50). The level of protection was not determined by price. It was determined by the regulator and was identical for everyone.

The filters split into two chemical families.

Organic (“chemical”) filters

These are carbon-based molecules that absorb UV radiation and convert that energy into a small amount of heat. The physics can be summarized in one sentence. A molecule absorbs a photon of UV and causes an electron to jump to a higher energy level. As the molecule returns to its ground state it releases that energy in the form of a small amount of heat instead of letting that UV reach and damage the DNA in your skin. These are referred to in older literature as “standard chemical filters.” Here is what that phrase actually means:

Inorganic (“mineral”) filters

These are the mineral filters. The old tag line is that they “sit on the top and reflect UV like a mirror.” This is mostly a myth. It has been found that these absorb UV like the organic filters do and scatter only a small amount. So the common statement that chemical filters absorb and mineral filters reflect is incorrect. Both families mainly absorb UV. The minerals are just big enough particles to also scatter a little visible light. This is also the reason they can cause a slightly white appearance.

Filter Protects against Legal max % Notes
Zinc oxide Broad: UVB + UVA2 + UVA1 25% The widest single-ingredient coverage in the toolbox. Gentle, reef-safer, leaves a slight white cast.
Titanium dioxide UVB + UVA2 25% Strong on UVB, weaker on long UVA. Often paired with zinc oxide.

The Photostability Problem — Formulation as Real Chemistry

There is a place where formulation truly matters. It is important to acknowledge this because it is chemistry, not a luxury. In American sunscreens, avobenzone is used as the UVA filter. However, avobenzone is photo-unstable. Photo-unstable means that some of the molecules of avobenzone, when absorbing UVA, do not return to the original form. Some of the molecules undergo a transformation into a new form that is less effective at absorbing UVA. In layman’s terms, avobenzone may partially self-destruct in sunlight. Some of the sun protection is lost in about an hour of sun exposure. If a sunscreen fails after an hour of beach time, it is worse than useless. It fails silently.

Solutions to this problem are quite simple and inexpensive. Pair avobenzone with some stabilizers. The most common of these are octocrylene, homosalate and octisalate. Octocrylene is a stabilizer that helps safely absorb and dissipate the excess energy that would otherwise cause the avobenzone to be photo-degraded. This allows avobenzone to remain in its original form while still providing sun protection. Homosalate and octisalate help with both stabilizing and dissolving the filters into an oil system.

What this means is that the difference between a good vs a bad chemical sunscreen can be in the stabilizing of the chemical sunscreen. This is a matter of formulation and is essentially free for low cost sunscreens. For example, when you see “avobenzone 3% · octocrylene · homosalate · octisalate” in a $1 sunscreen you are seeing a well-designed self-stabilizing UVA sunscreen. This is not a corner cut. It is the same trick the $48 sunscreen uses.

An Honest Nuance: the Chemistry Isn’t Fully Settled

The FDA re-evaluated these sunscreen filters in 2019–2021. Two of the filters, titanium dioxide and zinc oxide (mineral filters) were deemed to have sufficient data to be rated as GRASE: Generally Recognized As Safe and Effective. Twelve of the fourteen organic sunscreen filters (oxybenzone, octocrylene, homosalate, avobenzone, etc) were in a “needs more data” category, not deemed unsafe but not yet GRASE. Two filters, trolamine salicylate and PABA were deemed not-GRASE and essentially shelved.

Why? A team of scientists led by Matta conducted well-controlled studies to determine the amount of absorption organic sunscreen filters have into the blood. In these studies, participants used sunscreen under maximal-use conditions. It was found that some of the organic sunscreen filters are absorbed into the blood at levels greater than the FDA’s toxicological “threshold of concern.” This is a threshold below which a compound can be presumed not to need any further toxicological evaluation. This is the conclusion. This must be stated carefully. Absorption does not equal harm. Because a compound has been found in the blood it proves that the compound has been absorbed. It does not mean that it has caused any harm. The FDA decision was not that these compounds are harmful but rather that they “now need more data.” This is the appropriate FDA response.

The most important point here is that the uncertainty in sunscreen filters also applies to low cost and high cost sunscreens because they use the same filters. No one is getting out of this uncertainty by buying a more expensive sunscreen. The low cost mineral sunscreens, which are based on titanium dioxide and zinc oxide, seem to exist on the most safe end of this regulatory spectrum. These are also widely available for a few dollars.

The New Filter, and What It Reveals

The approval of bemotrizinol (also known as BEMT; or Tinosorb S outside the U.S.) in June 2026 best demonstrates this point. Bemotrizinol is a very photostable (i.e. does not degrade when in sunlight) and a relatively broad-spectrum sunscreen filter. It covers a wide range of both UVA and UVB wavelengths. It is also a stabilizer for other sunscreen filters. Bemotrizinol has been in affordable drugstore sunscreens since roughly 1999–2000 in Europe and in much of Asia.

For over two decades, bemotrizinol could not be sold in America in any price point. Not in a $5 sunscreen, not in a $50 sunscreen and not in a $150 sunscreen. One of the greatest improvements in sunscreen filters in the world has been gated not by price but by a regulatory process that was applied to everyone equally. When “better protection” finally came to America, it came to all sunscreens at once. Price was not the issue. Regulatory access was.

Here is the comparison that older tables hid behind a vague description. When looking at Drug Facts panels for both low cost and premium sunscreens they are almost the same four components present at almost the same concentrations:

The active ingredient in both, avobenzone, which is the sunscreen ingredient that does the UVA protection is at its maximum allowed concentration of 3% in both sunscreens. The same is true for the mineral sunscreens as well. A low cost mineral sunscreen such as CeraVe (~$8/oz) and a higher cost mineral sunscreen such as Colorescience (~$28/oz) are both based on zinc oxide. The active ingredient is the same mineral and costs roughly 3.5× more in the higher price sunscreen.

Mineral vs. Chemical Is a Real Choice — Just Not a Price One

A choice between a chemical or mineral sunscreen is a valid personal decision. There are real tradeoffs in this choice. However, these tradeoffs are not solved through price.

The problem of the white cast with mineral sunscreens and who it failed. Mineral sunscreens can leave a gray or ashy cast on the skin. By making the particles smaller, “nano” there is less visible light scattered. “Non-nano” particles are larger and less likely to cause concern with nanoparticle exposure. However, these are more visibly white. What is important here in terms of equity is that for those with darker skin the ashy or white cast may be actually unwearable. This has left mineral sunscreen as being quietly not meeting the needs of the people they were designed to be the gentlest to. This is a formulation challenge. Tinted sunscreens and better nano-dispersions are working to resolve this issue. However, this is a finish/texture issue. This is also an issue that both low cost and higher cost are attempting to resolve. It cannot be solved through price.

Reef safety. Two organic sunscreen filters, oxybenzone and octinoxate have been flagged in studies for potential harm to coral reefs. Hawaii and Key West have banned sunscreens containing these two filters. Mineral sunscreen (zinc oxide, titanium dioxide in non-nano forms) is generally considered to be “reef-safer.” Again this is a matter of which sunscreen filters you prefer. This can be found across all price points.

The One Free Factor That Dwarfs Everything

Now the most important sentence in this section. The most important factor in determining how well your sunscreen protects you is how much you apply and how often you re-apply your sunscreen. This is free and it does favor the lower priced sunscreen.

Sunscreen SPF is tested at approximately 2 milligrams per square centimeter. In practice this is about a shot-glass sized (about one ounce) amount of sunscreen applied to an adult and about a nickel to quarter sized amount applied to the face and neck. Studies have shown that most people only apply 1/4–1/2 of this. The cruel math for sunscreen is that the amount of protection decreases steeply, not in a straight line, as less sunscreen is applied. Applying half of the tested amount results in dramatically less SPF than half of the labeled SPF. A SPF 50 applied at half the amount may be equivalent to SPF 15 or less. Sunscreen must also be reapplied every couple of hours, and after swimming or sweating.

With all of this information in mind it can be seen that the person buying the $48 sunscreen has an incentive to only apply a small amount of sunscreen to the skin. They can stretch the sunscreen out. The person buying the $1 per ounce sunscreen has the ability to apply a generous amount of sunscreen frequently without concern about cost. They also can keep a spare in their car. Affordability does not mean a compromise in sun protection. It can be an advantage because the sunscreen behavior that leads to effective sun protection is to apply plenty and to re-apply often. Only the low cost sunscreen makes this behavior easy.

Sunscreen takeaway: Since the sunscreen active chemicals are capped by a federal law and, until 2026, were capped below what Europe allowed, for everyone alike, the higher priced $48 sunscreen cannot legally provide better protection than a well-formulated $1 per ounce sunscreen. Any real differences would be in finish, feel and appearance not UV protection. The most important factor in sunscreen, which is to apply enough and re-apply, is free and does favor the lower cost sunscreen. When sunscreen is available at an affordable price more sunscreen is used.

2. Moisturizer — Same Bricks and Mortar, Different Story on the Jar

Moisturizer is where the most famous price gap in skincare exists. The $16 tub of CeraVe (about a dollar an ounce) and the $200 per ounce Crème de la Mer. This is the perfect example because it reverses the popular perception. The drugstore moisturizer is actually based on a barrier-repairing compound that dermatologists support. Ceramides are these compounds. The luxury jar, as its own label will show, is not.

The Chemistry of “Barrier Repair”

Your skin has a protective outer barrier known as the stratum corneum. It is like a brick wall. The “bricks” are dead skin cells (corneocytes) that are flattened. The “mortar” between the bricks is composed of three types of lipids (fats): ceramides, cholesterol and free fatty acids. These lipids are laid out in layers known as lamellae. Ceramides are the most abundant of these lipids. The three lipids are present in a roughly balanced ratio. However, it was determined after decades of barrier research (especially research from Elias and Man and their colleagues on barrier repair) that what is important is not just the amount of these lipids present but the ratio of those lipids. If the ratio of lipids is off then the barrier does not form properly into the protective crystallized structure.

If the barrier is depleted or out of balance from aging, environmental conditions, over-washing or harsh products then the barrier becomes leaky. Water that should remain in the skin is lost to the atmosphere. This has been measured as trans-epidermal water loss (TEWL). Increased TEWL is what is at the biochemical level of “dry skin.” Dry skin is characterized by flakiness, tightness, roughness and irritation. The “barrier repair” is simply putting the three lipids back in the correct ratio. This repairs the barrier and TEWL decreases. This is the one moisturizer mechanism with strong, repeatable, peer-reviewed support, and it can be done affordably.

A ceramide is a fairly simple molecule. The structure has a long fatty acid chain and is attached to a sphingoid base (such as phytosphingosine) by an amide bond. Therefore the name cerAMIDe. The intimidating ceramide names on the label are all variations of this molecule:

Label name Older name Role in the wall
Ceramide NP (N-stearoyl phytosphingosine) Ceramide 3 Core barrier lipid; refills the mortar.
Ceramide AP Ceramide 6 II Adds structural stability to the lipid layers.
Ceramide EOP Ceramide 1 “Rivet” ceramide that locks the lipid sheets together.
Phytosphingosine — Building block the skin uses to make its own ceramides.
Cholesterol + fatty acids — The other two-thirds of the mortar mix.

The Three Jobs Every Moisturizer Mixes

Now step back and look at all moisturizers on Earth. Whether it is a $2 moisturizer or a $400 moisturizer there are essentially three things happening. If you learn these three terms you can decode any of the ingredients present in a moisturizer in seconds:

  • Humectants are molecules that help retain water. These molecules take moisture from the deeper layers of skin and from the air and pull it into the outer layers of the skin. Common humectants include glycerin (very cheap, very effective and is present in nearly all good skincare products) and hyaluronic acid (sodium hyaluronate). Regarding hyaluronic acid, brands love to advertise “multiple molecular weights” that sit at different depths in the skin. There is some science behind this. However, the advertising for it is very heavy. A simple sodium hyaluronate is doing the job of a humectant just fine. Glycerin, the humble one, is arguably the most reliable humectant there is.
  • Emollients are molecules that work to smooth and soften the skin. These molecules fill the gaps between cells. Emollients provide that “my skin feels nice” feeling. Common emollients include cetyl and cetearyl alcohols (fatty alcohols that are not the drying type of alcohols despite the name), caprylic and capric triglycerides, plant oils and butters. The most important note for emollients is that most of the “luxury” that a high priced cream provides comes from emollients and texture. The melt, the glide, the cushion, all come from the emollient system. It is real, it is pleasant and it does not help in barrier repair.
  • Occlusives are molecules that help lock in moisture by laying down a thin moisture barrier on top of the skin. This occlusive reduces the moisture from evaporating. The most affordable and effective occlusive that is also one of the best tolerated and least irritating skin compounds is petrolatum (petroleum jelly). It can reduce TEWL by the vast majority. It costs almost nothing. Dimethicone is a silicone that is a lighter, silkier occlusive that helps with texture as well.

A good moisturizer is a combination of a humectant to get moisture into the skin, an emollient to create a nice, smooth feel and an occlusive to lock in the moisture. Ideally this combination will also include ceramides, cholesterol and free fatty acids to actually help with barrier repair. None of these categories has a luxury tier. Petrolatum is petrolatum. Glycerin is glycerin.

The Receipt: What They Actually Share — and What La Mer Leaves Out

Side-by-side comparison of the two ingredient lists shows the opposite of what the prices imply. CeraVe Moisturizing Cream uses the full, well-researched barrier repair system. Near the top of the list are Ceramide NP, Ceramide AP and Ceramide EOP, cholesterol and phytosphingosine (a sphingoid base, the building block of ceramides), making up the dermatologist recommended ceramide-cholesterol-fatty acid complex. The formulation also includes hyaluronic acid, glycerin, dimethicone and petrolatum.

Compare this to the ingredients in Crème de la Mer. It contains algae (seaweed) extract, mineral oil, petrolatum, waxes, glycerin, lanolin alcohol, plant oils, some vitamins, a fragrance that contains limonene, citronellol, linalool, citral and other ingredients. It also contains mineral gluconates. But here is the part that should give you pause: there are no ceramides, no cholesterol and no phytosphingosine in the $200 product. The two products share only a few things in common: water, petrolatum, and glycerin. The world’s most famous “luxury barrier cream” does not have the barrier repair system that the $1 drugstore product is based around.

This inverts the premise. The drugstore product is not a diluted version of the luxury product. Instead it is the drugstore version that contains the barrier repair system that is missing in the high end product. What is the roughly 200× price difference for? The key ingredient in the product is called “Miracle Broth.” This is a fermented algae (sea kelp) extract. The Miracle Broth is also referred to as a proprietary healing formula. It is a fairly decent ingredient. Mineral oil and petrolatum are also effective occlusives. The product also has a nice feel to it. This is not snake oil. There is no independent, peer-reviewed evidence for barrier repair by this “broth.” This luxury line is also deficient in the ceramide-cholesterol-fatty acid complex which does have such evidence. You are buying into the myth, the frosted glass jar and the prestige but you are not receiving as much of the actual barrier repairing benefits found in the $1 per ounce drugstore product. You are also getting a fragrance that may irritate some sensitive skin. The only special thing about the luxury product is the myth. The luxury product is missing the barrier repair ingredients.

The Ironic Twist: the Budget Brand Holds the Patent

There is also a quietly satisfying part to this. The actually brilliant and actually patented part to this analysis is in the drugstore product.

CeraVe uses MVE delivery which is MultiVesicular Emulsion. This is a time released delivery system that is patented. The lipids are encapsulated in concentric layers (like an onion). As each layer is absorbed the next layer becomes exposed. This allows for the slow release of ceramides, niacinamide and fatty acids over a period of approximately 24 hours instead of all at once. This is addressing a real problem with a real solution. Barrier restoration is done gradually and effectively. Gradual lipid delivery may be more beneficial than immediate release of lipids. CeraVe was created in 2005 by dermatologists who believed the next advance in skin care would not be in a new active but in an improved delivery of existing, proven ingredients.

That is precisely what is occurring here. The drugstore product carries the proprietary delivery system. The luxury line carries the proprietary myth regarding kelp. One has put capital into a proprietary delivery system and the other has put capital into a proprietary kelp myth. The barrier repair system resides in the drugstore product at $1 per ounce. The luxury product sells the myth and not the barrier repair system.

Another key component worth touching on is niacinamide, a form of vitamin B3. Niacinamide can be found in CeraVe and many drugstore lines. Niacinamide is an incredibly mild, affordable and one of the best researched skincare ingredients available. Niacinamide also aids in ceramide synthesis in the skin. It can also aid in reducing redness and long term support of the skin barrier. Niacinamide is present in the $6 products because it is effective, not because it is inexpensive and nothing else is available.

Instead the high end product contains a proprietary “broth” which does not have evidence for barrier repair, plus added packaging and prestige. That does not mean that nothing is being done by La Mer. The product does contain effective occlusives (mineral oil and petrolatum) and it has a pleasant feel. It also does show that the barrier repair system is available in the drugstore product for $1 an ounce but the barrier repair system is not what is being paid for in the higher end products.

3. Cleanser — The Category Where Premium Makes the Least Sense

Sunscreen is the area where the law flattens the price differential. Cleansers are the area where physics and chemistry flatten the price differential. The function of a cleanser is accomplished by one category of ingredient. Cleansers are also on the face for less than 60 seconds before being rinsed away. These two aspects limit how much any price tier can deliver.

The Chemistry of Clean: Surfactants

Cleansers use surfactant chemistry. A surfactant (surface active agent) is a molecule with two parts. One part of the molecule is water-loving and the other part is oil-loving. This is also called an amphiphile. An amphiphile is a molecule that consists of a water-compatible (hydrophilic) part and an oil-compatible (hydrophobic) part. The key lies in the amphiphilic nature of the molecule. The grime that you are trying to remove from the skin, such as sunscreen, makeup, pollution and sebum, is mostly oil based. Oil and water do not easily mix.

Here is how this works, and it is simple. Surfactants will mix into water. When a certain quantity of surfactant is used (called the critical micelle concentration or CMC) the surfactants will spontaneously form spheres called micelles. In the micelle, the oil loving parts of the surfactant will congregate together and hide from the water. The water-loving parts of the surfactant will face outward. You now have millions of tiny oil friendly spheres suspended in water. When this product is applied to the skin the oil impurities will go into these spheres (like dissolves like). The impurities will get trapped in the micelles. The entire impurity-laden product will then be washed away with the water. This is the principle of a cleanser, whether it is for a $3 or $95 cleanser. The chemistry of a micelle does not depend on the product’s price.

Why Surfactants Differ in Gentleness

If the chemistry is the same, then why do some cleansers leave a soft feeling and others leave a “squeaky” feeling? This has to do with the fact that the harsher cleansers also attack the skin of the person. There are two problems associated with this:

  1. First, they remove the barrier lipids. A cleanser that is strong enough to remove oil will have difficulty making a distinction between the oil on the skin and the skin’s barrier lipid complex. Harsh cleansers will actually remove some of the skin barrier lipids along with the impurities. This undoes the repair work described in the moisturizer section and can actually increase trans-epidermal water loss and leave the skin dry.
  2. Second they can denature skin proteins. Certain harsh cleansers (especially the sulfates) can denature skin proteins. For example, the protein zein is used in lab experiments and can be denatured by strong detergents. When skin proteins are denatured by strong cleansers the skin can start to tighten up and become slightly swollen. This can give the “squeaky” feeling. This is not a “clean” feeling. This is mild damage.

Mild cleansers avoid both of these problems. Mild cleansers work near the skin’s pH. Mild cleansers remove oil without aggressively stripping the skin barrier lipids. Mild cleansers also largely leave the skin’s microbiome intact. Surfactants fall on a ladder from harsh to mild:

Surfactant family Examples Gentleness Behavior
Sulfates (anionic) Sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS), sodium laureth sulfate (SLES) Harsh Cheap, huge foam, strong cleaning — but strip ceramides, denature surface proteins, and raise water loss. The “tight, squeaky” feeling.
Amphoteric (mild co-surfactants) Cocamidopropyl betaine, cocamidopropyl hydroxysultaine Mild Coconut-derived, low-irritation. Boost foam and soften the harshness of stronger surfactants when blended in.
Amino-acid / isethionate / taurate Sodium cocoyl isethionate, sodium methyl cocoyl taurate, sodium lauroyl sarcosinate Gentle Clean near skin’s natural pH; clinically shown to preserve ceramides and the skin microbiome.
Glucosides (nonionic) Coco-glucoside, decyl glucoside Very gentle Sugar-derived, very low irritation; often in “sensitive skin” washes.

Notice that this does not have anything to do with price. The mild taurate, isethionates and the very mild glucosides are inexpensive and very widely available. A drugstore product that uses these mild surfactants makes a much better product than a luxury product that uses harsh sulfates to create a foamy feeling. Mild is a product choice and not a product price.

The Receipt: Budget and Luxury Share the Same Gentle Surfactant

Now decode the two ends of the market:

Product Tier $/oz Surfactant system (decoded)
CeraVe Hydrating Facial Cleanser Budget ~$0.92 Cocamidopropyl hydroxysultaine + sodium lauroyl sarcosinate + sodium methyl cocoyl taurate — all in the gentle tiers, no sulfates. Plus ceramides, hyaluronic acid, niacinamide.
La Mer The Cleansing Foam Prestige ~$22.62 Fatty-acid soap base (myristic, lauric, behenic, stearic acids) + sodium methyl cocoyl taurate + sodium coco PG-dimonium chloride phosphate.

The ingredient that is shared between the two is the same gentle taurate surfactant, sodium methyl cocoyl taurate that is in both the $0.92 drugstore product and the $22.62 La Mer foam. This is a difference of about 24× in price.

And here is the clincher that should end the discussion. The ingredient La Mer adds is not more advanced. It is older. The myristic / lauric / behenic / stearic acids used in the luxury product are soap chemistry. When these acids are combined with an alkaline base they will form soap via saponification. This is the traditional reaction from fat to soap. True soap tends to have a higher and more alkaline pH than the skin-friendly synthetic blend used in the drugstore product.

Recall the acid mantle from the primer. The acid mantle exists at a pH of ~4.7–5.5. True soap can be around pH 9–10 which is well above the comfort zone of the skin. This is exactly what would disrupt the acid mantle and cause a tight feeling. The drugstore CeraVe product uses syndets (synthetic detergents) which can be formulated to be close to the skin’s pH. CeraVe is thus less likely to disrupt the acid mantle. For the only attribute that actually matters to a cleanser (non-irritating to the skin barrier), the high-end product is in fact slightly more harsh than the other. You paid 24× more for soap formulation that your grandmother would definitely recognize.

Then Physics Finishes the Argument

Even without the above discussion, there is a limit that no cleanser can beat. Cleansers are on the skin for approximately 20–60 seconds before being washed off. Antioxidants, natural botanicals, precious ceramides and any other “leave on” components will most likely be washed away along with the other impurities before they can be absorbed. A serum used for several hours can provide some benefit that is not achieved by a cleanser used for less than a minute. For this reason, dermatologists show more agreement regarding cleansers than most other products. Avoid over investing in a facial cleanser. The upper limit on formulas is low for all consumers and not only on the low end. Physics does not have a luxury side.

A Fair Word on Micellar Water and “Double Cleansing”

Two popular practices need to be mentioned quickly. They are real and not snake oil. Also neither of these two practices require overspending.

  • Micellar water is a product which uses the same micelle chemistry discussed above but with a no-rinse base. It contains mild surfactants at low concentrations. It is applied with a cotton pad to remove makeup and impurities. It is a great product (for people with sensitive skin, travel, and lazy evenings). The effective versions are also relatively inexpensive. The chemistry is the same as any other cleanser.
  • Double cleansing is a technique that uses an oil-based cleanser first to remove makeup and sunscreen followed by a water based cleanser. This is a real technique if you are wearing heavy makeup or a long wearing sunscreen. Oil removes oil best thanks to “like dissolves like.” This is a technique and not a product tier. A cheap cleansing oil or even plain mineral oil is enough for the first step. Any gentle cleanser can do the second step. This is a technique and not a product price. The products are commodity cheap.

Cleansers take away: The same gentle surfactant based on coconut is used to remove impurities from the skin in both the $0.92 drugstore product and the $22.62 luxury product. In addition short contact time means that even the “best” cleanser barely outperforms a competent cheap product. This is the product category that has the least opportunity for a luxury price to make a difference. It is also an area where overspending is closest to overspending for nothing.

4. The Actives Where Attention (Not Money) Can Pay Off

Up to this point everything has said do not overspend. This is true and is the main point of this paper. However, an honest take should also add a more subtle point. Almost nothing in skincare rewards overspending. However, there are a handful of active ingredients that do reward attentive use. In every situation what rewards the user is either knowledge or medical access instead of a luxury price. These are the ingredients where an attentive and knowledgeable drugstore product user can outperform a wealthy user who just buys the most expensive product on the shelf.

Retinoids — The Real “More Is More,” Gated by a Prescription

The most well supported anti-aging actives in skincare are the retinoids, the vitamin A actives. To understand retinoids we will need one small ladder. Your skin can only use vitamin A in its final active form, which is retinoic acid. Lower forms of vitamin A must be converted up this ladder to retinoic acid:

retinol → retinaldehyde → retinoic acid

Each conversion on this ladder must be completed by the skin. Each conversion up this ladder results in some loss of potency. Retinol (the over-the-counter form of the ingredient in drugstore products) has to be converted twice by the skin before it can do anything. This is why it is gentler. Retinoic acid (prescription drug tretinoin) is in the final active form. No conversion is necessary. This is also why tretinoin is about 20× more potent than over-the-counter retinol (roughly: 0.05% tretinoin ≈ 1% retinol).

Here “stronger” does represent potency. This is one of the few instances of “more is more” in skincare. However, notice that the gate to stronger potency is a prescription and not a luxury price. Even this gate is beginning to fall. Adapalene (Differin) is a prescription strength retinoid. Differin went over the counter for approximately $13. Generic tretinoin is inexpensive. The only example of where potency can actually increase is through medical access rather than a luxury price. This again reaffirms the above thesis. A $13 Differin that is accessible at any pharmacy is more beneficial than a $200 high end retinol product because Differin uses prescription strength retinoids which are produced at a low cost. The high end product merely uses retinol in a high end package.

Vitamin C — Where Formulation Beats Prestige

Vitamin C (in the form of L-ascorbic acid) is a very beneficial ingredient. Vitamin C helps with collagen formation and can assist in brightening of the skin and also has antioxidant properties (helping neutralize UV and pollution induced free radicals). Vitamin C can also be an unstable ingredient. L-ascorbic acid can break down with water, light and air into inactive and yellow-brown compounds over time. If your vitamin C product has gone yellow or brown then it is most likely no longer effective.

This is not an issue of status. It is an issue of formulation and packaging. A good vitamin C serum hinges on things such as stability and absorption (pH), opaque or air limiting packaging, and stabilizers. These are more important than status. A cheap vitamin C serum formulated properly in an opaque container is often superior to an expensive vitamin C serum that has oxidized after being exposed to light. There are longer lasting, gentler and more stable forms of vitamin C that are somewhat less potent but sold at drugstore prices. However, freshness, storage in the dark, importance of knowledge and replacement upon color change over time are more important than price.

Niacinamide and the Acids — Cheap, Proven, Everywhere

Some of the most useful value skincare can be found on the “pay attention not prestige” list:

  • There has also been a lot of research into niacinamide (vitamin B3). This ingredient has been shown to help reduce sebum, calm redness and aid the skin barrier. Niacinamide is also relatively cheap and mild so is used in so many products.
  • Glycolic acid is an AHA (alpha hydroxy acid). AHAs are water soluble acids that help to break down the glue between the surface of skin cells allowing a smoother appearance and over time faster skin turnover. They are relatively affordable and well researched.
  • BHAs (beta hydroxy acids) e.g. salicylic acid: oil soluble so penetrate pores. Long standing proven results for blackheads and acne. Used in $8 products and effective.

What do they all have in common? The same optimistic tone of this paper. You do not need a high end product. The only ceiling is knowledge and knowledge is free. An informed consumer can differentiate between retinol and tretinoin (different strengths!) and know that vitamin C breaks down in the presence of light and that salicylic acid functions in the pore. Now you have all three.

Takeaways for actives: Some do require work but the work is in the knowledge. The hurdle is either a fresh product kept in the dark or a prescription. It’s not status. A knowledgeable $40 user can beat an unknowing $400 user.

If the chemistry is so similar, then where does the excess $40, $80 or $180 go? The economics of the beauty industry provide an answer. It goes into retail, marketing and margin, not into the product.

Value-Based vs. Competitive Pricing

The prices themselves are also a demographic choice. For a prestige product, value-based pricing is used. This means the price is determined not by the cost of the product, but by the value the consumer places upon experience and prestige. This gives rise to an $80 face cream. The $80 was not an arbitrary value, but was instead chosen because that is the value the target consumer would assign to that product based on the prestige and experience. Importantly, the higher the price the higher the value consumers assign to it because they view it as more expensive (price is in itself a form of prestige and can even be a placebo because the perception of an expensive product increases how effective it feels to consumers). For a budget product competitive pricing is used. The price is determined by competing products in the same category. A chemically equivalent product can be sold for $25. The price set depends on the target customer and not the cost of the product. Two products that are actually quite similar in formula may be set at vastly different prices (e.g. $25 and $80) because they are sold to very different types of consumers.

The Prestige vs. Mass Channel — and a Note on the “Pink Tax”

There is also a difference in the outlet for the product. In the prestige market (department stores and high end beauty retailers that are staffed, with samples, events and nice fixtures) the selling costs are relatively high. Recall that a significant amount of beauty marketing goes into experience and events. In the budget market (drugstores, big-box and warehouse stores) overhead is low, volume is high and savings are passed along. The extra money a buyer might have to pay in a department store is often in the service and the experience at the counter and not in the product.

There is also the so called pink tax. This is hard to measure, but also important. This is when a product that is targeted toward women costs more than an equivalent product that is targeted toward men (personal products like razors). There have been reports from consumer and government organizations on the pink tax with mixed results on different products and across different studies. Be careful with percentage differences. What we care about is that gender targeted products can have a price difference between essentially equivalent or equally chemically similar products. This same issue can be applied across a different axis. Compare across the aisle and even across men’s and women’s products. Let chemistry select and not marketing.

6. The Honest Counterpoint — Where the Inequality Is Real

A piece can be honest and not fall into the “everything is marketing and the low income consumer is fine” category. They are not. The issue at hand however is not where most would assume. It is not the cheaper moisturizer having inferior chemistry. The issue is exposure and access.

What “High-Hazard” Does and Doesn’t Mean

This point needs to be presented gently and in a kind manner since this can easily become fear. Fear is not the goal of this article. There are databases that assign a hazard score to a product according to its ingredients (for example EWG Skin Deep). A study by researchers at Rutgers and Columbia Mailman has also found that higher hazard ingredient usage is not distributed equally across all consumers. Middle aged consumers, Black women, and lower income consumers are more likely to use products with higher hazard ingredients. This may be due in part to the products that are offered and are commonly found in specific neighborhoods. (A very specific example of this has surfaced in this space: some hair products formulated for Black women have contained ingredients of concern. This has been known to researchers for many years.)

There are known ingredients which push hazard scores higher. For example, some phthalates (used as plasticizers and in fragrances) and some formaldehyde-releasing preservatives. These are avoidable ingredients. However, for this to be truthful and not fear inducing, there are two areas that can be addressed:

  1. Context and concentration are important. Some ingredients that are scary at high levels are actually not scary at low levels. Simply having a scary ingredient name (from the primer) does not imply that the molecule is scary. A hazard score implies the potential to cause harm, but does not always imply actual harm for a specific application.
  2. Safety does not necessarily equate to highest priced products. For example, a scented product containing many phthalates may have a fragrance free and more budget oriented safer alternative. This product is cheaper, not more expensive. In this case safety means not the ability to afford safety but rather which safer product can be obtained at a more manageable price.

The Real Barrier: Retail Geography

There is a structural issue. Deserts in pharmacy and/or retail (i.e. lack of pharmacy and/or retail with access to a sufficient number of safe and well formulated affordable personal care products) are more likely to occur in minority and low income communities (evident through spatial analysis of St. Louis and numerous scoping reviews). Effective ceramide moisturizer at $1 per ounce and effective mineral sunscreen does exist and is effective. They must also be on a shelf the consumer can physically reach and at a price the store in their community actually charges. They are not always available and/or are only available at a greater cost. If the closest store is a significant distance away and these products are not available and/or are only available at an increased cost then access to safe and affordable products is not achieved. This is an issue of access and availability, not a chemistry issue.

The Reframe — and Why It’s Hopeful

This reframing is useful to the argument and importantly is also presented in a kind manner. The problem is not a chemistry problem. Affordable, effective and safer products do exist and do work. The problem is that access to and availability of these products may not be equally accessible in minority and low income areas. This is a problem of access and exposure and not of “you must pay more to be safe.”

This is also not a personal failure. If access to products is limited by lack of stores nearby and product availability then this is a structural problem that needs to be addressed by society and not a knowledge or personal choice problem of the individual. The solution to this problem is not to tell individuals to buy La Mer. Instead the solution is more stores offering safer, better and more affordable products. Closing deserts and increasing product availability and making sure the democratic chemicals are available to everyone. The chemistry is on our side. All that remains is ensuring that these molecules are within reach of everyone. This is a fixable and hopeful issue that deserves to be named.

7. The Takeaway: You Don’t Have to Be Rich to Be Pretty

Across all three major steps of an effective skincare routine the results are similar and quite hopeful:

  • Sunscreen: Actives are capped by law so the cheaper product performs as well and since it is affordable there is a higher chance of being used in sufficient amounts.
  • Moisturizer: The science driven (ceramides, cholesterol and fatty acids) ingredients appear at the lower end of the price range. The popular higher end product omits these. The higher end product offers more of a story.
  • Cleansers: The same effective and mild surfactant is used in both the higher and lower end products and is rinsed off before price becomes a concern.
  • The actives that do require effort include acids, retinoids and vitamin C. These require knowledge and access and not a higher end product.

Skincare marketing implies that having a healthy glow is something that is purchased into. For example, that Kim K has this glow because of a $400 product that you cannot afford. However, the chemistry does not imply this. A ceramide is a ceramide. Avobenzone is avobenzone. Some of the most democratic skincare products are found at drugstores on the lower end of the skincare product price range. The true luxury lies in knowledge and not in the product. This knowledge is free.

The Biochemistry of Eating Well on a Budget — and Why “Healthy Food” Costs Less Than the Wellness Industry Wants You to Think

Healthy Choices Within Limits

Central Research Question: Do low-income consumers have to compromise on nutrition? Or is the difference between “healthy food” and “cheap food” an imposed distinction that is created by the food system and marketing, rather than by biochemistry?

Thesis: The biochemical components utilized by the body (fiber, fatty acids, vitamins, minerals, amino acids and others) are chemically the same whether they are in cheap or expensive foods. A dollar of lentils provides the same essential amino acids and the same vitamin C molecule as a dollar of organic “superfood”. Some of the most nutritious foods available are also some of the cheapest foods in the store.

Nutrition, however, is different from beauty in one way. There is a difference in food quality between whole and ultra-processed food. In this paper, that distinction is clearly named. The important point is that this distinction is not priced by biochemistry. The most nutritious foods are also some of the cheapest foods available in the store. The catch is that the cheapest calories by far are ultra-processed. This is why low income individuals are often drawn to ultra-processed foods. The true problems for low income consumers are infrastructure, time and access. They are not problems in the nutrient content of a food.

The Argument, in Plain Terms

One cup of cooked lentils costs approximately $0.30 and contains 16 grams of fiber and 18 grams of protein. A $14 bag of organic “ancient super-foods” provides the same classes of biochemical components that your body would break these down into anyway. A bag of frozen broccoli often contains as much or more vitamin C than the “fresh” broccoli that travelled five days to reach your grocery store. All of this is not hidden. It is printed on the Nutrition Facts label and is revealed in digestion.

This paper will follow the biochemistry from the cost of the food to the individual in the bloodstream to determine where nutrition ends and marketing begins and where the real problems for low income individuals are located. The good news comes early and stays. The biochemistry often favors the lower income consumer more than marketing for wellness would like you to believe.

What Your Body Actually Needs (a Two-Minute Biochemistry Primer)

Instead of consuming “foods” the body consumes biochemical components. Your body cannot read labels or prices of foods. Any food you eat is broken down into a set number of biochemical components. The system for breaking down any food is the same in every human being. No matter if the last meal for a human came from a food bank or a farmers market, the same system of breaking down food takes place.

How a Meal Actually Becomes You

It is useful to follow the journey of one bite of food. That is where the thesis for this paper is hidden.

In the mouth chewing serves two functions. First chewing breaks down food into smaller pieces. More surface area becomes available for enzymes to act on later. Second chewing mixes the food with saliva which contains an enzyme called amylase that breaks down long chains of starches into shorter chains of simple sugars. This is why a cracker can start to taste sweet if you keep it in your mouth. You are digesting it with your own saliva.

In the stomach food undergoes a bath of hydrochloric acid (pH between 1.5 and 3). This acid is also strong enough to dissolve metals and stronger than either vinegar or lemon juice. The acid also performs an important function in relation to protein. The acid can cause proteins to denature. Denaturing occurs when a tightly wadded-up protein is unfolded into loose strands, the way heat unravels an egg white from clear to opaque. When a protein is denatured it causes the seams of the protein to be exposed. The acid in the stomach also activates an enzyme called pepsin which starts to break apart exposed proteins into shorter protein chains. None of this depends on what the protein cost.

In the small intestine proteins undergo a different level of breakdown. More enzymes are added from the pancreas. Proteases help break down proteins into individual amino acids. Lipases break down fats into fatty acids. Amylase breaks down carbohydrates into individual sugars such as glucose. Bile from the gall bladder serves as a built in surfactant which helps break up fat into smaller droplets so that the lipases can act on them. The wall of the small intestine is covered by millions of finger-like structures (villi) that help move glucose, amino acids, fatty acids, vitamins and minerals into the blood and lymph.

When food from an individual’s meal enters the bloodstream it is no longer considered to be “steak” or “lentils”. Instead it is a mixture of identical biochemical components. The body does not learn the cost of any food.

The Building Blocks Themselves

Everything your body needs sorts into two big families.

Macronutrients — the energy and the structure, the stuff you eat in gram-sized amounts:

  • Protein → broken down into 20 amino acids 9 of which are “essential” meaning the body cannot make these and they must be consumed. The amino acids are then used to build muscle, antibodies, enzymes and hormones.
  • Carbohydrates → broken into glucose which is a quick source of energy for the body and fiber which is a type of carbohydrate that your body cannot break down. Fiber enters the large intestine undigested and becomes food for the gastrointestinal flora (more on this later).
  • Fats → broken down into fatty acids. Two fatty acids, omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids are “essential” in the same way that amino acids are. This means the body cannot produce them and they must come from the food we eat. Fats also carry the fat soluble vitamins into our bodies.

Micronutrients — the spark plugs, needed in milligram or microgram amounts but non-negotiable:

  • 13 vitamins (A, C, D, E, K, and the eight B vitamins), and
  • a set of essential minerals — iron, calcium, potassium, magnesium, zinc, iodine, and more.

Vitamins fall into two types which have very different behaviors. For those looking to consume nutrition cheaply this is important.

  • Water soluble vitamins (all eight B vitamins and vitamin C) dissolve in water. These vitamins are stored in very small amounts and the excess is excreted. Since these vitamins dissolve in water and are also fragile they are easily lost during cooking or by time, light and heat. (This is also why “fresh vs frozen” foods play out the way they do later.)
  • Fat soluble vitamins (A, D, E and K) dissolve in fat. These vitamins can be stored in fat and the liver for weeks or months. Fat soluble vitamins also hold up better during cooking. A small amount of fat is required to absorb fat soluble vitamins. This is why a little oil on your vegetables is not a “treat”. It is also required for the absorption of vitamin A contained in that $0.25 carrot.

Two Rules That Carry the Whole Paper

Rule 1: Nutrients are defined by their molecular structure not the food they come from. Vitamin C is ascorbic acid. Ascorbic acid is a specific arrangement of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen atoms. Ascorbic acid is the same whether it is found in a $0.30 frozen vegetable or a $8 cold pressed juice. The body has specific enzymes and receptors shaped for the ascorbic acid structure. These enzymes and receptors will bind to the ascorbic acid as well regardless of its source. A molecule itself does not have a story or a history. A molecule has a structure. That is all that is relevant to the biochemistry.

Rule 2: Digestion is the great equalizer. Whether it is a $40 steak or a 15 cent rice and lentils dish, digestion breaks both down to the same free amino acids and those amino acids are then absorbed into the bloodstream. All of the digestion outlined above applies equally to both high cost and low cost foods. Once a protein is digested, the origin of that protein becomes unimportant. Let’s now see this in action with the single most hyped nutrient in the supermarket.

Primer takeaway: Food is eaten as molecules. All food goes through a uniform digestion pathway. In the stomach it uses acid and pepsin and in the small intestine it uses enzymes and bile. All foods are broken down into sugars, fatty acids, micronutrients and amino acids and then absorbed. Two important rules arise: the importance of the molecule of a nutrient and the equalization created by digestion between high and low cost sources.

1. Protein — The Most Over-Marketed Macronutrient

Protein is the nutrient most heavily marketed by the health and wellness community: grass-fed steak at $40; protein powders at $60; “clean” this and “grass finished” that. However, the science behind all of this is somewhat deflating.

The Chemistry: It’s All Just Amino Acids

A protein is a long chain of amino acids. There are 20 amino acids found in proteins. 9 of these amino acids are essential (histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, valine). Imagine these 20 amino acids as a 20-letter alphabet. All proteins in the world, including the proteins in a steak (collagen), your hair (keratin) and the proteins found in a legume are all “words” composed of these same letters.

All of the proteins that you consume get broken down by your digestive system into these 20 amino acids. This is the important point: once digested, the origin of the protein is irrelevant. The leucine found in a ribeye is the same molecule as the leucine in a chickpea. This same leucine performs the same function in your body. Your body can then use this common supply of amino acids to create its own words (its own enzymes, its own muscle, etc). Your body does not care and cannot differentiate between a leucine obtained from grass finished beef or from a package of dry lentils.

Completeness and the “Limiting” Amino Acid

One exception to this is that of completeness. A “complete” protein contains all nine of the essential amino acids in a reasonable proportion (eggs, dairy, meat, fish, soy). Most single plant sources of protein are “incomplete”. It is important to understand just what that means however because it is less of a problem than it appears.

Imagine that you are building a word that requires all 20 letters of the alphabet. If you had all 20 letters except for one letter that you only had in small quantities then that one letter would limit the number of complete words that could be created. This letter is referred to as the limiting amino acid. In grains the limiting amino acid is lysine (grains run low in it). In legumes and beans the limiting amino acid is often methionine. Neither is completely lacking however each is relatively lacking.

What is interesting is that legumes and grains are low in different amino acids. Grains are low in lysine but contain adequate methionine. Legumes are low in methionine but contain adequate lysine. Placing these two foods together or even in the same day can allow each food to make up for the lack of one of the other foods. Rice and beans, lentils and bread, hummus and pita, peanut butter on toast: the combination of proteins is complete and can be purchased for pennies. Many different cuisines have discovered these food pairings through hunger, taste and the foods that were grown locally long before any amino acid was named. The chemistry simply confirms what has already been discovered by grandmothers.

Correcting an Old Myth: You Don’t Have to Combine at the Same Meal

For many years nutritionists have given plant protein eaters a problem. Vegetarians have to combine complementary proteins within the same meal. Beans and rice together but not apart. This idea comes from a popular book (Diet for a Small Planet, by Frances Moore Lappé published in 1971.) This has caused plant protein to become a stressful problem.

The author has later retracted this statement and it is important to note this because it makes eating cheaply far easier. The body keeps a pool of free amino acids in circulation throughout the day. At lunch you could eat rice and at dinner you could eat beans. The lysine and methionine would still be available to build complete proteins. You do not need to combine complementary proteins within each meal. You only need a variety of proteins throughout the day. Some beans and legumes, some grains. Perhaps some dairy or an egg. The stressful combination of proteins was never necessary.

How Much Protein Do You Actually Need? (A Concrete, Cheap Anchor)

Marketing tends to prey on the fear that individuals are not “getting enough” protein. How much protein is needed however is the following. The RDA (Recommended Dietary Allowance) for most adults is approximately 0.8 grams of protein per kg of body weight per day. For a 70kg (approximately 154 pounds) adult this works out to about 56 grams of protein per day. For people who are athletes, people who do heavy strength training and older adults (who use protein less efficiently) more protein may be needed. Often this amount is cited to be between 1.2 and 2.0 g/kg however even this can be reached easily and cheaply.

How quickly can cheap foods get you to this amount? Three eggs (~18 g) + one cup of milk (~8 g) + one cup of lentils (~18 g) is already 44 grams before any meat, protein supplements or protein “enhanced” foods are added. This is for under a dollar of protein. Enough protein is not expensive. It has never been.

The Leucine Threshold — the One Detail Muscle Actually Cares About

There is one specific type of muscle protein biochemistry that the supplement industry likes to play upon. Of all the amino acids one, leucine, is the “on switch” for muscle protein synthesis. There is evidence that a meal must clear a leucine threshold of about two grams of leucine to strongly turn on this “switch”.

The implied conclusion of the supplement industry is that expensive isolated supplements are necessary to clear this threshold. The actual conclusion is that complete protein foods can clear this threshold. Eggs, dairy products and milk, tofu/soy and a good amount of beans and grains contain enough leucine per meal to turn on muscle protein synthesis. A glass of milk and a serving of beans over rice are performing the same function as a $60 tub of supplements.

How Nutritionists Actually Score Protein Quality (and Why It Doesn’t Rescue the Premium Story)

If someone claims that premium sources of protein are “higher quality” then ask what they mean by that. There are two scales that scientists use and neither of them correlate to the price of a protein.

The previous standard was PDCAAS (Protein Digestibility-Corrected Amino Acid Score). It scores proteins based on content of all nine essential amino acids in adequate proportions and digestibility. Egg, dairy, and soy protein score high (near or at 1.0), and are used as the reference proteins for all other proteins. Notice that two of the three highest scoring proteins (dairy and soy) are also among the least expensive proteins you can buy in the store.

The new and more accurate system is DIAAS (Digestible Indispensable Amino Acid Score). It measures the amount of each individual indispensable amino acid digested in the small intestine. DIAAS provides slightly better ratings for some animal proteins and slightly more conservative ratings for some plant proteins. However, the bottom line does not change: egg and dairy proteins are all reference quality and inexpensive combinations of plant proteins (grains and beans or simply a variety of plant sources) can yield the complete profile. Both systems score complete proteins with adequate digestibility. Neither scores on the words “grass fed” or “designer whey.”

The Premium-Meat Pitch, Decoded

The premium protein story is “grass fed/organic.” Does this affect the protein? No. Grass-fed beef contains slightly more omega-3 fats but the protein, in terms of amino acid composition, remains unaffected. For a slight difference in fat content and a picture of grass on a label, you can pay two to three times more for organic over regular beef. This does not provide any benefit in terms of protein. A combination of lentils and rice provides your muscles the same nine essential amino acids as a $40 steak.

“Designer whey” deserves a call out as a perfectly clear example of this situation. Whey is the by-product created from milk when used to make cheese. It has historically been a problem to dairies since they had to dispose of it. Luckily for us, whey is a complete protein. The $60 whey in the matte tub with the athlete on it and the bulk whey from the warehouse is frequently the same product. The differences between these are in price, marketing, and flavoring and not in the amino acids that get absorbed into your muscles.

Protein Takeaway: Muscle is built from amino acids in your body that you cannot track back to a specific product. The cheapest complete protein options (eggs, milk and grains and beans) provide the same essential amino acids as premium meats but at 3 to 5 times the value per dollar. The quality scoring systems that are actually used by nutritionists (PDCAAS and DIAAS) designate egg, dairy and soy as reference proteins that are also inexpensive products. You do not need to combine proteins at the same meal or reach a certain level of leucine with a supplement to increase muscle. “Grass-fed,” “clean” and “designer whey” are marketing and do not alter protein composition.

2. Micronutrients — Vitamins, Minerals, and the Fortification Secret

If protein is over-marketed then micronutrients are mis-marketed as “organic,” “cold-pressed” or “superfood” when these molecules could be found in frozen food at a fraction of this cost.

A Vitamin Is a Molecule, Not a Brand

Vitamin C is ascorbic acid, one specific molecule. Whether you get this ascorbic acid from a 30 cent frozen vegetable or from an $8 artisanal juice, it will bind to the same enzymes you have in your body. Same goes for folate, iron, calcium, and potassium. This fact alone dismantles most of the price gap in the produce aisle.

But there is an important qualifier to the statement above: the chemistry that affects the ability of a molecule to be absorbed into the body (bioavailability) can be controlled by you and is completely free.

The Bioavailability Chemistry Worth Learning

Iron: heme and non heme. Iron comes in two forms. Iron found in meat, poultry and fish is largely heme iron. This iron is contained in a ring-shaped molecule which is well absorbed (about 25%). Iron from beans, lentils, spinach and fortified foods is non-heme iron. This iron is absorbed with less efficiency (17% or less and frequently less than that). This does appear to give an advantage to expensive meat until one learns that there is a cheap solution.

Vitamin C helps with plant iron. Vitamin C reduces non-heme iron into a form that can be absorbed more efficiently in the intestine and also helps keep this iron soluble. In short, pair your beans, lentils, or spinach with a food that is high in vitamin C. Tomatoes, peppers and citrus juice will do, as will salsa. This can dramatically increase the amount of iron you absorb for pennies. A pot of lentils with tomatoes is not only a tasty meal, but also a lesson in bioavailability.

Absorption inhibitors and how to cheaply defeat them. Some plant compounds bind minerals and inhibit their absorption:

  • Phytates (phytic acid) which are found in whole grains, beans, nuts and seeds bind calcium, iron and zinc.
  • Oxalates which are found in spinach and other plants bind calcium and iron. (This is also why spinach is a mediocre source of calcium. Much of the calcium is bound up by oxalates.)
  • Tannins in tea and coffee bind iron.

Defeating these is achieved with cheap kitchen practices. Soaking and cooking beans and grains decreases the phytates found in these foods. Cooking can also partially break down oxalates. Sipping tea or coffee between meals instead of with meals will help prevent tannins from interfering with the absorption of iron found in your food. All of this is cheap. What it costs is a little know how. This is exactly what this series is trying to give you.

Cooking has both benefits and drawbacks. Some water-soluble vitamins are destroyed by high heat. Vitamin C and folate are lost into cooking water and also degraded by heat. This is why steaming or microwaving is better at preserving these vitamins than boiling for longer periods of time. However, there are also benefits to cooking. Cooking breaks down cell walls and makes certain nutrients available that were previously unavailable. Cooked tomatoes release more lycopene (an antioxidant) than raw tomatoes. Cooking also makes beta-carotene found in carrots more available. Cooking also helps break down phytates and oxalates mentioned earlier. There is not a blanket statement that raw is more nutritious. The chemistry varies for each nutrient and in many cases cooked food can be the healthier choice.

Fresh vs. Frozen vs. Canned: The Chemistry Favors Cheap

Here is the counterintuitive part. “Fresh” is not the same as “nutrient-rich.”

Why do vitamins in “fresh” food disappear? A picked food is still somewhat alive. The cells within the foods continue chemical reactions. The enzymes in the fresh food as well as warmth, light and oxygen also break down certain vitamins. Vitamin C and folate are water-soluble vitamins as discussed in the primer. These degrade with time, light and heat after the food is picked. Spinach and broccoli can lose up to 75% of their vitamin C within a week of being picked while in trucks and on store shelves. The term “fresh” refers to where the food is sold and not the amount of vitamins it contains.

Frozen foods differ from fresh in that they are flash frozen at peak ripeness within hours of being picked. This stops degradation of certain vitamins almost immediately and freezes the enzymes and reactions in the food. A study done by UC Davis (Bouzari, Holstege & Barrett, 2015) found frozen foods to have similar or sometimes higher levels of some vitamins compared to fresh-stored foods. This is because flash freezing occurs soon after the food is picked which halts degradation. However, for a few vitamins such as beta-carotene there can still be a loss. The result is that frozen produce can be as nutritious if not more nutritious than “fresh” foods that have traveled for several days. The food was picked at a higher ripeness and nutrients were preserved sooner.

Canned foods follow a similar trend with a slight difference. While some heat sensitive vitamins can be reduced by the canning process it also helps break down cell walls and make certain nutrients available. This is why canned tomatoes can release more lycopene than raw tomatoes. Canned beans, canned tomatoes and canned fish are all shelf stable, inexpensive and provide nutrition. The frozen and canned food aisles are not the compromise foods. They are some of the best in terms of nutrition per dollar in the store.

The Fortification Secret: Nutrients Engineered Into Cheap Food

One of the great public health advances of the 20th century is not being reflected on a food label. The government has added vitamins and minerals to some of the cheapest foods in the store either by law or by standard. Every pound of enriched flour must contain thiamin, riboflavin, niacin, iron and folic acid (0.7 mg) by federal standard. The results are historic and chemical and it is important to highlight each one. Each one was a disease that was causing widespread illness and even death:

  • Niacin added to flour → nearly eliminated pellagra, a B3 deficiency that was responsible for dementia, diarrhea and death in the American South during the early 20th century.
  • Folic acid fortification (since 1998) → has dramatically decreased neural-tube defects such as spina bifida by ensuring that people of reproductive age get folic acid whether or not they actively seek it.
  • Iodized salt → almost eliminated iodine deficiency and goiter which is one of the cheapest public health measures ever taken.
  • Vitamin D added to milk → eliminated childhood rickets, a disease of vitamin D deficiency that caused deformities in the bones of children.

The point is that some of the least expensive foods in the store (fortified cereals, enriched flour, a gallon of milk and iodized salt) are built by design to provide micronutrients. You do not have to overpay to get safety. The floor has been raised for everyone by design and decades ago. It is still there today.

“Organic” and “Superfood,” Decoded

The biggest premium in this category is organic. Does it provide more nutrition? The most comprehensive review to date (a 2012 meta-analysis in the Annals of Internal Medicine by Smith-Spangler et al. from Stanford) combined 17 human studies and 223 studies on nutrient and contaminant levels. There was not a statistically significant difference in nutrients between organic and conventional foods. Organic does reduce exposure to pesticides but conventional pesticide residues remain within safe levels. Nutritionally the “organic” premium is comparable to La Mer in skincare. It provides a real story, a real price tag but does not provide a different molecule that gets delivered to your cells. (Organic can be a legitimate value for farm worker safety, environmental impact or pesticide reduction but not as a claim that a carrot provides more vitamin A.)

“Superfood” has no scientific or legal definition at all. It is purely a marketing term. It can be printed on any product by anyone. A 25 cent carrot and a 25 cent bag of frozen spinach contain the same classes of antioxidants as a $12 açaí bowl. The açaí bowl is a tasty food. It is not a chemically better food.

Getting the Most From Cheap Nutrients (the Honest Caveats)

Bioavailability offers cheap wins but intellectual honesty requires acknowledging three areas in which the chemistry sets a hard limit:

  • Iron, pair it with vitamin C. As mentioned earlier the heme iron found in meat is absorbed at about 25% while non-heme iron found in beans, lentils and spinach is absorbed at 17% or less. Vitamin C can help convert plant iron into a form that is more available for absorption. Pairing beans with citrus juice, peppers or tomatoes can dramatically increase the amount of iron that is absorbed for pennies.
  • Vitamin B12, a hard limit. Vitamin B12 is naturally found only in animal products (meat, fish, eggs and dairy) and is not found in plants. This is not marketing. It is chemistry. If you do not consume animal products then you must either consume a fortified food or a cheap supplement. There is no other way around this. Fortunately, there are cheap options for this. Fortified plant milk, nutritional yeast and generic B12 supplements are all inexpensive.
  • Omega-3, fish beats seeds. The plant omega-3 fatty acid (ALA) found in walnuts and flax must be converted by the body into the active forms found in the brain and heart (EPA and DHA). The conversion rate is only about 5–20% for EPA and less than 4% for DHA. This is why a $1 can of sardines that provides EPA/DHA is a better and cheaper omega-3 than more expensive “superfood” seeds. Again cheap quietly wins on the chemistry.

The Cheapest Nutrient Powerhouses

Ranked by nutrition per dollar the cheapest and humble options win. These are the dietary equivalent of drug store ceramide creams:

Micronutrient takeaway: A vitamin is a fixed molecule. The frozen and canned sections are where micronutrients actually reside and not the organic section. Frozen often beats “fresh” because flash freezing helps preserve nutrients “fresh” foods lose in transit. Fortification provides nutrients in the cheapest products for free. “Organic” and “superfood” provide a story and not a chemically better product. Some cheap tricks like pairing beans with vitamin C, soaking and cooking to defeat phytates, keeping coffee or tea away from meals and opening a can of sardines can help get even more out of the value shelf.

3. The One Real Catch — Ultra-Processed & Fast Food

Here is where a different answer is required from Part 1. In skincare cheap is not chemically worse. In food there is one type of food that can chemically be worse and cheaper: ultra-processed foods. This section is where compassion is needed so let’s be exact with the chemistry and careful with the blame.

The NOVA System: What “Processed” Actually Means

Nutrition science categorizes foods not by calories but rather by the level of industrial processing. The NOVA classification (Monteiro et al.) identifies four categories with accurate sorting preventing a common reaction of fear. “Processed” is a spectrum and only the far end is chemically problematic:

  • Group 1, Unprocessed or minimally processed: Foods in their most natural form. Fresh, frozen or dried vegetables and fruits; milk; eggs; meat; dried beans and lentils. (Freezing and drying are forms of minimal processing and do not affect the healthfulness of a food.)
  • Group 2, Culinary ingredients: Substances derived from group 1 that are used in cooking. Sugar, salt, vinegar, butter, oil. These are used to cook food but are not consumed by themselves.
  • Group 3 — Processed foods: Group 1 foods with Group 2 ingredients added for preservation or flavor, still recognizable as food: canned beans, cheese, freshly baked bread, canned fish, canned tomatoes. Most of the cheap powerhouses in this paper live in Groups 1 and 3, and they are genuinely good for you.
  • Group 4 — Ultra-processed foods (UPF): industrial formulations built mostly from refined fractions and additives you’d rarely find in a home kitchen — high-fructose corn syrup, hydrogenated oils, modified starches, protein isolates, emulsifiers, and cosmetic additives for color, flavor, and texture. Soft drinks, packaged snack cakes, most breakfast cereals, reconstituted meat products, many ready meals.

The crucial move: the problem is concentrated in Group 4. A can of beans and a bag of frozen broccoli are “processed,” and they’re excellent. The concern isn’t processing in general. It’s ultra-processing.

The Chemistry of Why Ultra-Processed Food Harms

Chemically, here is what makes Group 4 a problem:

Refined carbohydrates. Milling strips the bran and germ from whole grain, removing the fiber, B vitamins, minerals, and healthy oils, and leaving mostly fast-digesting starch — the white endosperm. That starch floods the blood with glucose quickly (a high glycemic load), which spikes insulin, the hormone that shuttles glucose out of the blood. The insulin spike can overshoot, dropping blood sugar and leaving you hungry again soon after. You ate, but your body’s fuel signal whipsawed, and now you want more.

Added sugars. Table sugar (sucrose) and high-fructose corn syrup are both essentially glucose plus fructose. The glucose half raises blood sugar; the fructose half is processed largely in the liver, and in excess it drives the liver to manufacture fat. Sugar also delivers calories with zero micronutrients — the textbook “empty calorie.” It provides energy and nothing to build with.

High sodium and industrial fats; low fiber and micronutrients. The package is engineered to be energy-dense and hyper-palatable — formulated to a “bliss point,” the precise combination of salt, sugar, and fat that maximizes crave and encourages you to keep eating past fullness. This isn’t an accident; it’s the product working as designed.

A note on emulsifiers and the gut. Ultra-processed foods often contain emulsifiers — additives that keep oil and water mixed so a product stays smooth and shelf-stable. Some laboratory and animal research suggests certain emulsifiers may disturb the gut lining and the microbiome (the community of bacteria in your intestine). This evidence is still emerging and mostly from animal and cell studies, so it should be held loosely — but it’s part of a growing picture that how a food is built, not just its nutrition label, may matter.

Fiber and the Microbiome — the Thing UPF Strips Out

Remember the fiber your own enzymes couldn’t digest? It travels to your large intestine, where trillions of gut bacteria ferment it and produce short-chain fatty acids — molecules that nourish the cells lining your colon, help regulate inflammation, and appear to support metabolic health. Fiber is, in effect, food you eat for your bacteria so they can make something useful for you.

Whole foods — beans, lentils, oats, vegetables, whole grains — are packed with this fiber. Ultra-processing strips most of it out. So Group 4 food doesn’t just spike your blood sugar and outrun your fullness signals; it also underfeeds the microbial ecosystem that helps keep you healthy. This is another mechanism where the cheap whole foods in this paper — beans, oats, frozen vegetables — quietly do something the engineered food cannot.

The Evidence It’s Real: The Hall et al. 2019 NIH Trial

This is not hand-waving. In a tightly controlled 2019 NIH metabolic-ward trial (Hall et al., Cell Metabolism), 20 adults lived on a research ward and ate freely from either an ultra-processed or an unprocessed diet — and here’s the rigorous part — the two diets were matched for calories offered, sugar, fat, fiber, and sodium. On paper, the nutrition labels were equivalent.

They were not equivalent in the body. On the ultra-processed diet, people ate about 508 more calories per day and gained weight; on the whole-food diet they lost it. A major reason was mechanical: participants ate the ultra-processed food about 17 calories per minute faster. Soft, energy-dense, easy-to-chew engineered food goes down quickly — and the gut’s “I’m full” hormones take roughly twenty minutes to reach the brain. Eating faster means emptying the plate before the satiety signal arrives, so you overshoot before your body can say stop. The processing itself, not just the numbers on the label, changed how much people ate. That is a landmark finding, because it isolates the one variable — industrial processing — that the wellness-vs-junk debate usually confounds with everything else.

One honest note to keep the science clean: beyond this controlled trial, most of the broader evidence linking ultra-processed diets to disease is observational (associational) — large population studies that find UPF-heavy diets track with worse outcomes but can’t fully prove cause and effect on their own. The Hall trial is powerful precisely because it was a controlled experiment. The direction of the evidence is consistent and concerning; we should state its strength honestly and not overclaim.

The “Fast Food Is Cheaper” Question — Honestly

The defense of fast food is that poor people eat it because it is the affordable option. The honest answer has two layers, and skipping the first one is how well-meaning advice turns into victim-blaming.

Layer one: per calorie, cheap processed food really is the cheapest. Nutrition economists (led by Adam Drewnowski) have shown for years that the lowest-cost source of raw energy is refined grains, added sugar, and added fat — the exact building blocks of ultra-processed food — while fresh produce, lean meat, and fish are the most expensive way to buy a calorie. A Harvard meta-analysis (Rao, Afshin, Singh & Mozaffarian, BMJ Open, 2013) found the healthiest diets cost about $1.50 more per person per day — roughly $550 a year — than the least healthy ones. So when someone short on money simply needs to not be hungry, reaching for dense, cheap calories is a rational response to the prices in front of them, not a failure of willpower. Hunger is immediate; the long-term cost of empty calories is abstract; a person choosing to feed their kids tonight is doing the math correctly for the situation they’re in.

Layer two: per nutrient — and per full, satisfying meal — whole foods win decisively:

Meal ~Cost per serving What you get
Fast-food combo meal ~$11.50 Refined carbs, added sugar, high sodium, low fiber
Home pot of rice & beans ~$1–2 Complete protein, fiber, folate, iron, potassium
Pot of lentil soup ~$0.25–0.33 18 g protein + 16 g fiber per bowl

A single combo meal can cost more than a week of lentil soup, and delivers a fraction of the protein, fiber, and micronutrients. The resolution of the two layers: eating well on a budget is absolutely possible, but it requires buying nutrients rather than the cheapest calories — and that swap costs something other than money. It costs time, planning, equipment, and a store that stocks the right foods. Which is exactly the real barrier, and the subject of Section 5.

Ultra-processed takeaway: Ultra-processed food (NOVA Group 4) is the one place where a cheap option is genuinely worse for your health — its refined-carb, added-sugar, low-fiber, hyperpalatable chemistry drives overeating even in a controlled experiment where the nutrition labels were matched. It is the cheapest source of calories, which is why a tight budget rationally bends toward it — but it is not the cheapest source of nutrients or the cheapest full meal. Dried beans, lentils, rice, oats, eggs, and frozen vegetables beat it on nutrition and on cost-per-meal. What fast food actually sells is time and convenience — and that is the thing poverty makes most expensive.

4. So Why Does “Healthy” Feel So Expensive?

If nutritious molecules are cheap, why does “eating healthy” feel like a luxury? Two forces, one cultural and one industrial, layer an expensive feeling on top of a cheap reality.

The Cultural Force: Health Sold as Identity

The wellness market sells health as an identity. “Organic,” “grass-fed,” “superfood,” “clean,” “cold-pressed,” “artisanal” — these are value-based pricing attached to the same amino acids and vitamins, exactly like luxury skincare charging for a story. Value-based pricing means the price is set by what the story is worth to your self-image, not by what the ingredients cost to produce.

The supplement aisle takes it further. It sells you vitamin C and fish-oil molecules — the very same ascorbic acid and EPA/DHA discussed above — at a steep markup over the ordinary food that already contains them, often in doses your body simply flushes out (remember: the water-soluble vitamins aren’t stored; excess vitamin C mostly leaves in your urine). You can spend real money buying, at a premium, molecules that a can of sardines and a bag of frozen broccoli would have handed you as a bonus.

Underneath all of it: being seen to eat well has become a status signal. The glass jar, the matte label, the farm photo, the word “artisanal” — these advertise that you are the kind of person who can afford to care. Status is the actual product. The nutrients came free with the groceries.

The Industrial Force: Ultra-Processed Food Wins the Shelf

Meanwhile, the food industry engineers ultra-processed food to win the shelf. Refined ingredients are cheap, shelf-stable, and high-margin; tuned for craveability, they sell more per square foot than a sack of dried beans ever could. So the most heavily marketed, most widely stocked, most convenient foods are disproportionately the ultra-processed ones — not because whole food is more expensive to grow, but because processed food is more profitable to sell.

Put the two forces together and you get the paradox you feel at the register: at one end of the store, whole nutrients are quietly cheap but poorly marketed and sometimes poorly stocked; at the other end, a premium “wellness” tier charges luxury prices for the same molecules with a better story. The pricing you feel is a marketing-and-distribution artifact layered on top of biochemistry that quietly favors the cheap staples. The chemistry never got more expensive. The story around it did.

5. The Honest Counterpoint — Where the Inequality Is Real

This is the section that keeps the paper honest, and it matters even more than its twin in Part 1. Saying “nutritious food is cheap, so just cook beans” would be both true and useless — even cruel — if it ignored why so many people can’t. The inequality is real. It is simply not located in the price of the nutrients. It is located in access, time, and infrastructure.

The real barrier Why it’s structural, not personal
Food deserts & food swamps By USDA data, roughly 53.6 million Americans (17.4%) live in low-income areas far from a supermarket; many neighborhoods have convenience stores and fast food but no full grocery selling cheap dried beans or fresh produce.
Time poverty A pot of beans is cheap in dollars but expensive in hours. Soaking, simmering, and cleanup assume time and energy that someone working two jobs or raising kids alone may not have. Fast food sells that missing time.
Upfront cash & storage (the “poverty premium”) The cheapest per-unit prices require buying in bulk — which needs cash on hand, a car to haul it, and a pantry/freezer to store it. Buying small to survive the week means paying more per ounce.
Kitchen & equipment access Cooking from scratch assumes a working stove, pots, refrigeration, and reliable utilities — not guaranteed in unstable or temporary housing.

Reading the Barriers Closely — Without Blame

Look hard at that table, because each row is a place where the cheap-food strategy quietly assumes a resource that poverty strips away.

A food desert is a neighborhood where the nearest full grocery store is far enough that reaching it without a car is a genuine ordeal; a food swamp is the more common and more insidious version — a place saturated with convenience stores and fast food but with nowhere nearby to buy a bag of dried lentils or a head of broccoli. When the store on the corner doesn’t stock the cheap powerhouses, “just buy lentils” is advice for a store that isn’t there. That’s a stocking-and-distribution failure, not a shopping failure.

Time poverty is the quiet one. This entire paper has praised beans, lentils, and oats — and every one of them takes time: soaking, simmering, chopping, cleanup. Time is not free, and it is most scarce for exactly the people with the least money — someone working two jobs, or parenting alone, or commuting hours on a bus. Fast food’s real product, as Section 3 concluded, is time, and poverty is what makes time most expensive. Reaching for the fast option isn’t laziness; it’s triage.

The poverty premium is a cruel arithmetic: the per-unit prices this paper celebrates — the cheap bulk bag of rice, the big sack of dried beans — require upfront cash, transport, and storage to unlock. If you can only spare a few dollars today, can’t haul twenty pounds home on foot or by bus, and have nowhere to store it, you’re forced to buy small — and buying small costs more per ounce. The poor literally pay higher unit prices for the same food. The discount belongs to people who already have cash, a car, and a pantry.

And kitchen access is the assumption underneath all the others. Every “just cook it” tip silently assumes a working stove, pots, a fridge, and reliable utilities — none of them guaranteed in unstable, temporary, or shared housing. You cannot batch-cook beans without a pot and a working burner.

The Structural Fixes (Because the Frame Is Hopeful, Not Hopeless)

Naming these barriers isn’t a counsel of despair — it’s a map of what to fix, because every one of them is structural, which means it has a structural solution. None of this requires anyone to have more willpower. It requires society to stop making it so hard:

  • Grocery access — incentives and infrastructure that bring full-selection grocery stores, and fresh and frozen produce, into food deserts and swamps.
  • Income and time — living wages and humane hours give people back the two resources (cash and time) that turn cheap ingredients into meals.
  • SNAP and produce-incentive programs — food assistance (SNAP) raises the grocery budget, and produce-incentive programs that stretch benefits further when spent on fruits and vegetables have shown they can shift what people are able to buy. These meet people where the prices actually pinch.
  • School and community meals — feeding kids and neighbors directly bypasses the whole kitchen-and-time bottleneck for the people most exposed to it.

This reframes the thesis exactly as Part 1 did. The inequality is not a biochemistry ceiling — cheap, nutrient-dense food exists and works. It is that turning cheap raw ingredients into meals demands time, infrastructure, and stability that poverty systematically strips away. That is a structural problem with structural fixes — grocery access, living wages, time, school and community meals, produce incentives — not a lecture about willpower, and not a reason to buy organic.

6. The Takeaway: You Don’t Have to Be Rich to Be Healthy

Two truths, held together, are the honest answer:

  • The nutrients are democratic. Amino acids, vitamins, minerals, and fiber are cheap, and the foods richest in them — eggs, beans, lentils, oats, frozen vegetables, canned fish, milk — cost pennies per nutrient. Your body cannot tell the difference between a budget molecule and a boutique one, because after digestion there is no difference: the leucine, the ascorbic acid, the calcium are identical whatever carried them in.
  • The barriers are structural. Where low-income eaters lose ground, it is to food deserts, time poverty, and missing kitchens — and to an economy where the cheapest calories are the unhealthy ones — not to a price tag on the nutrients themselves. Fixing that is society’s job, not the individual’s failing.

The wellness industry runs on the same quiet suggestion as the beauty industry: that health is a thing you purchase your way into, that the glowing, well-fed life belongs to people who can afford $14 superfoods and grass-fed everything. The biochemistry says otherwise. A complete protein costs about 30 cents. A vitamin is a vitamin. The molecules that build a healthy body are among the most affordable products in the entire grocery store. The genuine luxury is not the organic label — it is the access, time, and knowledge to use cheap food well. The knowledge, at least, is free — and this paper is one attempt to hand it over.

The One-Line Answer to the Research Question

Good nutrition under financial constraints is biochemically possible — the molecules your body needs are among the least expensive things in the store, and a nutritious diet can be built for very little. The real inequality is not the price of nutrients but the access, time, and infrastructure needed to turn cheap food into meals — and the fact that the cheapest calories are the unhealthy ones. You do not have to be rich to be healthy — but society does have to stop making it so hard to be poor and healthy at the same time.

The Argument in One Paragraph

American teenagers are in the middle of a documented mental-health crisis, and it is worst among girls. A major engine of that crisis is appearance pressure, delivered through social media — feeds full of edited, filtered, idealized images that we judge ourselves against, often without realizing the images are fake. That pressure has a price tag: the beauty and wellness industry sells the “solution” back to teens at premium prices, which means the students with the least money get the most hopeless version of the deal. But the research is just as clear about what helps: teaching young people to see through idealized media and appearance marketing measurably improves body image and builds lasting skepticism. That is exactly what this project is. The gap between a $1-an-ounce moisturizer and a $200-an-ounce one is marketing, not chemistry — and learning that fact is a free, evidence-aligned defense against the exact pressure making teens miserable.

1. The Crisis Is Real — and It’s Worst for the Students This Project Serves

The CDC’s Youth Risk Behavior Survey is the gold-standard federal survey of US high school students, run since 1991. Its most recent published results, from the 2023 survey:

  • 40% of US high school students felt so sad or hopeless almost every day for at least two weeks that they stopped doing their usual activities — up from 30% in 2013.
  • 53% of girls reported persistent sadness or hopelessness, versus 28% of boys — a 25-point gap. Over the decade, girls rose from 39% to 53%.
  • 1 in 5 students seriously considered attempting suicide in the past year (girls: 27%; boys: 14%).
  • The CDC’s own summary: “Nearly all indicators of poor mental health and suicidal thoughts and behaviors worsened from 2013 to 2023.”
  • At the 2021 peak, 57% of teen girls — nearly 3 in 5 — felt persistently sad or hopeless, roughly double the rate for boys.

Zoom out with federal health statistics and the picture sharpens: adolescents 12–19 have the highest depression prevalence of any age group — 19.2%, about 1 in 5. Depression runs a steep income gradient: 22.1% below the federal poverty line versus 7.4% in the highest-income group (ages 12 and up) — roughly three times higher for the poorest households. And only about 4 in 10 people with depression received professional mental-health care in the past year.

The students facing the most appearance-driven distress and the least access to care are precisely the ones a free, knowledge-first project is built for.

2. Appearance Pressure Is a Mental-Health Mechanism — Not Vanity

Social media is where modern appearance pressure lives, and its reach among teens is total. Up to 95% of teens 13–17 use it; more than a third use it “almost constantly”; the average 8th–10th grader spends 3.5 hours a day there. In the CDC’s 2023 data, 77% of high school students use social media several times a day — and frequent users were 1.35× as likely to report persistent sadness or hopelessness. The US Surgeon General’s 2023 advisory cites a study of 6,595 adolescents in which those spending more than 3 hours a day on social media faced double the risk of depression and anxiety symptoms.

And teens themselves name the mechanism — it’s appearance:

  • 46% of teens say social media makes them feel worse about their body image (only 14% say better). Surgeon General, 2023
  • 43% feel pressure to only post content that makes them look good to others.
  • 34% of teen girls say social media makes them feel worse about their own lives (vs. 20% of boys), and girls are about twice as likely as boys to say it has hurt their mental health or confidence. 48% of all teens now say social media has a mostly negative effect on people their age — up from 32% two years earlier.
  • The American Psychological Association’s formal conclusion: appearance-related social comparison online is “related to poorer body image, disordered eating, and depressive symptoms” — especially for girls.

Even Instagram’s own internal research — leaked in 2021, later released by Meta — reached the same conclusion about its own product. One internal slide stated plainly: “We make body image issues worse for one in three teen girls.” In a second internal study, 67% of US teens reported feeling bad when they felt pressure to look a certain way — and 38% felt bad about pressure to buy certain things Meta deck 2. Hold on to that last number; it’s where this project comes in.

3. The Images Are Literally Fake — and We Can’t Tell

This is the sharpest experimental evidence in the whole literature, and the part most people don’t know.

In a randomized experiment, 144 girls (ages 14–18) viewed either original or secretly retouched-and-reshaped Instagram selfies. Girls shown the manipulated photos reported significantly lower body satisfaction — a direct causal effect, not a correlation. The girls could not reliably detect the editing: they noticed filters, but the digital slimming of waists and legs went largely unnoticed, and they rated the reshaped photos just as realistic as the originals. Worse, the fake images won the comparison — they were rated significantly more attractive — 4.6 versus 3.4 on the study’s attractiveness scale. The ideal that teens measure themselves against literally does not exist.

Related research shows it’s the editing, not the screen time: among young teen girls who share selfies, photo manipulation and photo investment — not overall media exposure — predicted body-image and eating concerns. And editing hurts the editor too: in a 2020 experiment, the act of taking and editing a selfie increased negative mood and facial dissatisfaction — the more participants edited, the worse they felt.

The clinical endpoint of this pipeline has a name. “Snapchat dysmorphia” — coined in JAMA Facial Plastic Surgery (2018) — describes patients seeking cosmetic surgery to look like the filtered versions of themselves. In the facial-plastic-surgery industry’s own annual surveys, the share of surgeons seeing patients who wanted to look better in selfies rose from 42% (2016) to 55% (2017) to 72% by 2019.

4. The Pressure Has a Price Tag — and That’s Where Inequality Bites

The beauty and wellness industry doesn’t just benefit from this pressure. It sells the cure. US teens’ annual “core beauty wallet” hit $339 in 2024, the highest since 2018 — and US households with children 12 and under spent $2.4 billion on skincare and makeup in 2023, more than households with teens. Dermatologists warn the “Sephora kids” trend is medically harmful: children’s skin is thinner and more sensitive, and the anti-aging actives being marketed to them can cause real damage.

Now put the pieces together. The pressure is near-universal. The marketed answer costs money — and is aimed younger every year. Depression already runs about three times higher below the poverty line, and most affected people get no professional care. A low-income student gets the full dose of the pressure, can’t afford the “solution,” and has the least access to help.

And the punchline of this entire project is that the trap is fake. As the Skincare and Nutrition papers on this site show, molecule by molecule, the expensive “solution” is chemically near-identical to the affordable one. The premium is marketing. Which means the way out isn’t money — it’s knowledge.

5. What the Evidence Says Actually Helps

School programs that teach teens to deconstruct idealized media and appearance marketing have been tested in randomized trials for two decades. The results:

  • The APA’s official recommendations point straight at this approach. Its 2023 advisory recommends adolescents limit social media use built on appearance comparison, and receive training in media literacy.
  • The Body Project — a program where teen girls actively argue against the appearance ideal — cut the onset of eating-disorder pathology by 60% over 3 years in its flagship randomized trial. A meta-analysis of 56 randomized trials (7,808 participants) backs the approach, and it works when school nurses and counselors — not researchers — deliver it.
  • Dove’s school program “Confident Me” improved body esteem in cluster-randomized trials and reduced appearance-based teasing among girls up to a year later; it has reached 18.6 million students in 45 countries. One key lesson: the single-session version’s effects faded — repetition matters.
  • Media-literacy programs specifically: a 2022 meta-analysis of 17 school-based studies found small but significant body-image improvements — and the largest, most durable gain was in media-literacy skill itself, which persisted after body-image effects faded. Reviews agree the effective ingredients are media literacy, self-esteem, and peer-influence activities, and that these programs reliably build skepticism about media realism and awareness of profit motives.
  • Even the free behavioral lever works: in a randomized experiment, college students who capped social media at ~30 minutes a day for three weeks showed significant drops in loneliness and depression. And a 4-year study of 3,826 adolescents found depression symptoms rose in the years teens used social media more — with the authors pointing to upward comparison with idealized images as the best-fitting explanation.

Honesty requires the counterpoint: one analysis of 355,358 adolescents found overall digital-technology use explains at most 0.4% of the variation in well-being. The fair reading of the full literature: raw screen time is a weak predictor — but the appearance-comparison pathway specifically (edited images, appearance-focused feeds, photo investment) is where the consistent harm shows up. That is exactly the pathway this project targets.

6. Where This Project Fits — the Honest Impact Case

Line this project up against the evidence and the fit is almost exact:

  • It’s media literacy for wellness marketing. The APA formally recommends media-literacy training; the research shows it durably builds skepticism about media realism and profit motives. This project’s core teaching — the price gap between budget and luxury wellness is marketing, not chemistry — is a profit-motive deconstruction, applied to the exact product category where teen appearance pressure is monetized.
  • It contains the single most effective ingredient: arguing against the ideal. The strongest programs work by having students actively make the case against appearance ideals. Reading an ingredient label and articulating why the $200 cream isn’t better than the $1 one is that argument.
  • It defuses the pressure to buy. 38% of US teens feel bad about pressure to buy things — per Instagram’s own research — while teens spend $339 a year on beauty. The message that better isn’t actually being sold attacks the idea that pays for all that pressure — the idea that expensive means better.
  • It’s built for the students hit hardest. Depression is about three times more prevalent below the poverty line, and professional care reaches a minority. Free knowledge, in school, meets students where the need is greatest.

What a project like this can credibly aim for, calibrated against the trial literature: durable gains in marketing skepticism (the outcome that lasts), small short-term improvements in body image (real, but modest and fade-prone — which is why this should be a repeated conversation, not a one-off assembly), and less “I can’t afford to be pretty or healthy” distress, replaced with verified knowledge.

What this project will never claim: that it treats or prevents depression, or substitutes for professional care. The evidence supports it as a prevention-aligned educational effort on the appearance-pressure pathway — a real contribution, honestly sized.
